**BACKGROUND**
<br>
New York City's street network accommodates millions of commuters, commercial vehicles, transit lines, pedestrians, and micromobility riders daily. Under the city's Vision Zero safety initiative, the New York City Department of Transportation (DOT) works to eliminate traffic fatalities and severe injuries through data-driven road redesigns, targeted enforcement, and public policy. Using official NYPD motor vehicle collision open data — spanning crash timestamps, geographic locations, contributing factors, vehicle classes, and casualty figures — we evaluate multi-year safety patterns to assess roadway danger, identify primary crash drivers, and provide actionable evidence to guide mayoral traffic safety priorities.

<br>

**TARGET AUDIENCE**
- **NYC Department of Transportation (DOT) Leadership and Engineers:** To guide street redesigns, signal timing adjustments, and infrastructure capital allocation.
- **NYC Mayor's Office and Policy Advisors:** To support citywide legislative proposals, funding requests, and strategic Vision Zero messaging.
- **NYPD Traffic Enforcement Division:** To optimize law enforcement deployment, speed camera placement, and targeted traffic enforcement hours.
- **Urban Planners and Road Safety Advocates:** To identify regional safety disparities, evaluate vulnerable road user risk, and inform community board planning.

<br>

**QUESTIONS TO BE ADDRESSED BY THE ANALYSIS**
1.	**Collision Trends and Temporal Dynamics**
- Are motor vehicle collisions across NYC increasing or decreasing over time?
- How do crash volumes fluctuate by year, month, day of the week, and hour of the day?

2.	**Geographic and Spatial Distribution**
- Which boroughs bear the highest volume and density of traffic collisions?
- What are the top 10 most dangerous corridors and intersections across the five boroughs?

3.	**Contributing Factors and Behavioral Drivers**
- What are the primary contributing causes of collisions citywide?
- How do leading crash causes vary across different boroughs and corridor types?

4.	**Casualty Risk and Vulnerable Road Users**
- Which user groups—pedestrians, cyclists, or motorists—face the disproportionate share of severe injuries and fatalities?
- How have injury and fatality rates per collision evolved over time?

5.	**Vehicle Class Involvement and Crash Severity**
- Which types of vehicles (e.g., passenger sedans, SUVs, heavy commercial trucks, motorcycles) are involved in the highest number of crashes?
- Are specific vehicle types disproportionately linked to severe injuries or higher fatality rates?

6.	**Policy and Intervention Strategy**
- What targeted policy, enforcement, and infrastructure interventions should the NYC DOT prioritize to improve roadway safety?

**COLLISION TRENDS & TEMPORAL DYNAMICS**

**Total Crashes Per Year**

<img width="1189" height="686" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/31cbdaa8-e3e5-4ee6-999b-1294bd796609" />


Looking at the full-year data from 2018 through 2023, **the total number of traffic crashes in New York City is significantly decreasing**:
-	**Peak Period (2018):** Crashes peaked at 231,563 incidents.
-	**Major Drop (2020):** During the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, **crashes dropped sharply by nearly 47% to 112,916**.
-	**Steady Decline (2021–2023):** Rather than rebounding to pre-pandemic levels as traffic returned, **crashes have continuously fallen each year** – reaching **96,570 in 2023 (a 58% total drop compared to 2018).**

<br>

**Key Implications for NYC DOT**
- **Safety Policies Are Working:** Traffic calming efforts (such as Vision Zero initiatives, speed camera expansions, bike lane additions, and lower speed limits) appear to be having a positive compound effect.
- **Shift in Travel Patterns:** Post-pandemic remote/hybrid work models have reduced overall daily vehicle volume during peak commuting hours, contributing to fewer opportunities for collisions.
-	**Focus Shift Required:** While total crash volume is down, lower congestion can lead to higher vehicle speeds on open roads. DOT’s primary risk focus must shift from frequent fender-benders to high-speed severe injuries and fatalities.

<br>

**Actionable Recommendations**
1.	**Protect Vulnerable Road Users:** DOT should prioritize physical infrastructure (e.g., concrete-protected bike lanes and daylighting intersections) for pedestrians, cyclists, and micro-mobility users (e-bikes/scooters).
2.	**Target High-Crash Corridors:** DOT should concentrate street redesign budgets on the remaining top 10% dangerous intersections rather than citywide blanket redesigns.
3.	**Redesign Wide Arterial Streets:** DOT needs to implement road diets  and automated speed enforcement on multi-lane avenues where open traffic flow encourages speeding.

**Peak Risk Hours/Crash Volume by Hour of the Day**

<img width="1189" height="687" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/86ddcd35-d64f-4040-8b3a-f737f63af009" />

**Interpretation of Hourly Crash**
1. **Workday Commute and School Rush (8 AM – 9 AM & 2 PM – 6 PM)**
- **Morning Peak:** On weekdays, collisions jump starting at 8:00 AM as commuters heading to work mixed with families dropping children off at school congest streets simultaneously.
- **Afternoon/Evening Peak:** The highest concentration of crashes occurs late in the afternoon (2:00 PM to 6:00 PM/14:00 to 18:00). Traffic volumes hit their highest levels during these hours due to overlap between school dismissals, commercial deliveries finishing up, and evening commutes.
  
2. **Friday’s 4 PM to 5 PM Spike (16:00 – 17:00)**
- Friday between 4:00 PM and 5:00 PM records the single highest crash total of the entire week **(11,219 collisions)**.
- **Why it happens:** This peak **is not primarily caused by clubbing or parties**. Instead, it is driven by early weekend departures, weekend trip traffic mixing with normal evening commutes, higher delivery traffic, driver fatigue at the end of the workweek, and impatience in heavy gridlock.
  
3. **Overnight Hours (Midnight to 5 AM) and Late-Night Weekend Risk**
- **Saturday 0:00 (Midnight):** Saturday’s midnight hour (0:00 to 1:00 AM) is directly connected to Friday night out activities.
- **Saturdays and Sundays (0:00 – 5:00 AM):** While overall traffic volume drops late at night, early morning crash numbers on Saturday and Sunday are roughly **1.5 to 2 times higher than early morning crashes** on weekdays (e.g., 6,700 crashes at midnight on weekends vs. 5,500 on weekdays).
- **Alcohol and Impairment Factor:** Late-night weekend crashes heavily reflect people returning from social events, restaurants, bars, and clubs. Reduced traffic speeds combined with alcohol or drug impairment lead to a higher proportion of severe and fatal crashes during these early morning hours.

<br>

**Key Recommendations**
1.	**Optimize Traffic Signal Timing for Peak Commute Windows**
- Adjust traffic signal phases (e.g., longer pedestrian lead times and split-phase turns) between 2:00 PM and 6:00 PM on high-incident corridors to manage evening traffic congestion and protect pedestrians.
2.	**Expand 24/7 Automated Speed and Safety Enforcement**
- Maintain continuous round-the-clock speed camera operations, particularly targeting known high-speed corridors during the 12:00 AM to 5:00 AM weekend window when open roads encourage dangerous driving.
3.	**Targeted Late-Night Transit and Safety Interventions**
- Partner with late-night venue districts and rideshare/transit agencies to improve safe, accessible late-night transportation options, reducing impaired driving on Friday and Saturday nights.
4.	**School Zone and Commercial Loading Enhancements**
- Implement strict curbside loading regulations during peak afternoon hours (2:00 PM to 5:00 PM) to clear delivery trucks double-parking near schools and busy intersections.

**GEOGRAPHIC AND SPATIAL DISTRIBUTION**

**Crashes by Borough**
<img width="1189" height="690" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/39f1d310-68b7-4e36-a2de-c744003d6041" />

When we look at traffic collisions across New York City, crashes are heavily concentrated in two main boroughs: **Brooklyn and Queens**. Together, **these two areas account for more than half (56.4%) of all reported crashes in the city.**

<br>

**Which areas have the highest rates?**
-	**Brooklyn (Highest Risk):** Ranks #1 with **308,791 total crashes (approx. 29.4% of all collisions)**.
-	**Queens (Second Highest):** Follows closely at #2 with **282,847 total crashes (approx. 27.0%)**.
-	**Manhattan and The Bronx (Moderate Risk):** Stand in the middle tier with **183,039 crashes (17.5%) and 163,916 crashes (15.6%)**, respectively.
-	**Staten Island (Lowest Risk):** Accounts for the fewest collisions by a wide margin, with **46,374 crashes (4.4%)**.
-	**Unspecified Locations:** About **6.1% (63,608 crashes)** lacked borough coordinates in the records.

<br>

**Key Implications for NYC DOT**
- **Vast Geographical Spread vs. Density:** Brooklyn and Queens cover large geographical areas with complex mixes of high-speed commercial avenues, dense residential neighborhoods, and major highway feeder corridors.
- **Disproportionate Resource Allocation:** Applying an equal "one-size-fits-all" budget split across all five boroughs would under-fund the areas where nearly 60% of traffic incidents occur.
- **Data Logging Gaps:** The 63,600+ "Unspecified" borough records mean police officers at crash scenes occasionally fail to log crucial spatial metadata, making localized safety planning harder in those specific cases.

<br>

**Actionable Recommendations**
1.	**Prioritize Brooklyn and Queens Infrastructure Budgets:** Allocate at least 55% to 60% of roadway safety, street redesign, and traffic-calming budgets specifically to high-crash corridors in Brooklyn and Queens.
2.	**Redesign Multi-Lane Commercial Avenues:** Focus street redesigns (e.g., adding pedestrian islands, dedicated turn lanes, and protected bike paths) on major thoroughfares in Brooklyn and Queens that connect residential neighborhoods to highways.
3.	**Standardize NYPD Reporting Protocols:** Work with local law enforcement to ensure electronic crash reporting automatically captures precise GPS location coordinates to eliminate unclassified crash logs.

**VEHICLE CLASS INVOLVEMENT AND CRASH SEVERITY**

**Vehicle Types and Crashes**

<img width="1366" height="689" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/0a363233-1fc4-4e12-ac2e-f4381c98ec90" />


<br>

An analysis of crashes categorized by vehicle type highlights the following breakdown:
- **Passenger Vehicles Dominate Crash Numbers:** **Sedans (48.6%)** and **Station Wagons/SUVs (37.4%)** account for **86% of all recorded crashes combined (859,318 total incidents)**. Everyday personal passenger cars drive the vast majority of collisions on NYC streets.
- **Commercial and Service Vehicles Form a Secondary Tier:** **Taxis (4.1%)**, **Pick-Up Trucks (2.8%)**, and **Box Trucks (2.0%)** represent the largest non-passenger vehicle categories involved in crashes.
- **Vulnerable Modes Appear Less Frequently in Total Volume:** **Bikes (1.3%)** and **Motorcycles (0.7%)** account for a small fraction of overall collisions. However, when these smaller vehicles are involved, outcomes tend to be disproportionately severe.

<br>

**Key Recommendations**
- **Target Personal Passenger Driver Behavior:** Expand automated speed and red-light camera enforcement in residential and commercial corridors where sedan and SUV traffic is concentrated.
- **Redesign Loading Zones for Delivery Trucks:** Establish dedicated curb loading zones for box trucks and pickup trucks to reduce double-parking hazards and side-swipe collisions on busy avenues.
- **Enhance Direct-Vision Standards and Vehicle Safety Advocacy:** Advocate for safety standards that improve driver visibility in large SUVs and light trucks to reduce blind-spot impacts with pedestrians and cyclists.
- **Physically Separate Vulnerable Road Users:** Continue building physically protected bike lanes to isolate cyclists from the massive volume of sedans and SUVs on shared streets.
